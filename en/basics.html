Investment Basics

What do you get when you buy a stock? How can you tell if a price is "high" or "low"? What are dividends and compound interest? Here are the basics to know before you think about investing — with simple examples.

What is a stock?

Economics in 30 seconds

A stock is a small piece of a company

Companies need a lot of money to build stores and factories. So they issue "shares" and collect money from many people. If you buy shares, you become a shareholder — one of the company's owners.

For example, if a company has 10,000 shares and you own 100, you own 1% of the company.

What shareholders can get ①

Capital gains: if you sell after the price goes up, the difference is your profit. (If it goes down, you lose money.)

What shareholders can get ②

Dividends: part of the company's profit paid out to shareholders. More

What shareholders can do

Vote at the shareholders' meeting, where important company decisions are made. More shares means more votes.

Why do stock prices move?

Prices are set by the balance between buyers and sellers. When many people expect a company's profit to grow, more people want to buy and the price tends to rise. But if good news was already expected, the price can fall even after strong results.

What are P/E and P/B ratios?

Rulers that compare the price with profit or assets

P/E ratio = price-to-earnings

Share price ÷ profit per share
It shows how many years of today's profit the price equals.

Example Price ¥2,000, profit per share ¥100 → P/E is 20×. If profit stays the same, it takes 20 years of profit to equal the share price.

P/B ratio = price-to-book

Share price ÷ net assets per share
Net assets are what the company owns minus what it owes. P/B shows how many times that the price is.

Example Price ¥2,000, net assets per share ¥1,600 → P/B is 1.25×. Below 1× means the market values the company at less than its net assets.

Watch out

A low P/E or P/B does not always mean "cheap." It may be low because people expect profit to fall. Normal levels also differ by industry, so compare companies in the same industry, or a company with its own past.

What are dividends?

A dividend is part of a company's profit shared with its shareholders. Many companies pay once or twice a year (some pay four times). You receive an amount based on how many shares you own.

Dividend yield = yearly dividend per share ÷ share price

Example Price ¥2,000 and a yearly dividend of ¥60 per share → the dividend yield is 3%. With 100 shares (¥200,000), you receive ¥6,000 a year (before tax).

Not guaranteed

If business gets worse, dividends can be cut or stopped.

There is a deadline

You must be listed as a shareholder on the company's "record date."

Taxes apply

In Japan, dividends are usually taxed at about 20%. In a NISA account they are tax-free (FSA NISA site, Japanese).

How compound interest works

Economics in 30 seconds

Interest that earns interest = compound interest

Put ¥1,000,000 in at 5% a year and you earn ¥50,000 in the first year. With simple interest, you only earn interest on the first ¥1,000,000, so it grows by ¥50,000 every year. With compound interest, the interest is added to your money, so in year 2 you earn 5% of ¥1,050,000 = ¥52,500, and so on.

The difference is small at first, but it grows like a snowball over many years.

Method: compound interest grows by 1/12 of the yearly rate each month (monthly savings added at month end). Simple interest is only paid on the starting money and the amounts saved. Taxes and fees are not included. Real investments do not grow at a steady rate and can fall below what you put in.

The rule of 72

"72 ÷ yearly rate (%)" gives the rough number of years for money to double. At 6% a year, 72 ÷ 6 = about 12 years. At 3%, about 24 years.
