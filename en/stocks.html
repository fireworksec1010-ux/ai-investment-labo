What is a stock index?

A stock index combines the prices of many companies into one number. It is like the "class average" on a test: without checking every company one by one, you can see whether the market as a whole is doing well.

Economics in 30 seconds

Why look at an index?

One company's stock price can move a lot because of things that only affect that company. An index that combines many companies shows how "Japanese companies," "U.S. companies" or "companies around the world" are doing overall. Many investment funds are built to follow an index.

Nikkei 225 (Nikkei Stock Average)

Calculated from 225 leading Japanese companies

Calculated from 225 companies listed on the Prime Market of the Tokyo Stock Exchange. Companies with a higher price per share move it more. In Japanese news it is reported in yen, like "the Nikkei is at ○○ yen."

TOPIX (Tokyo Stock Price Index)

A wide range of companies on the Tokyo Stock Exchange

An index of a wide range of companies listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange. Like the S&P 500, bigger companies (by market value) move it more. It includes more companies than the Nikkei 225, so it is said to be closer to the whole Japanese market.

All-Country also contains many U.S. companies, so the two often move in similar ways. The difference is whether you also spread your money across countries outside the U.S. Which is better depends on each person's thinking. Country weights change over time, so check the official site for the latest.

3 things to watch when reading index numbers

① With or without dividends

Some versions of an index include the dividends companies pay to shareholders and some do not. The version with dividends shows bigger numbers.

② In dollars or in yen

The S&P 500 is calculated in dollars. If you invest from Japan, exchange rates matter too: it can rise in dollars but fall in yen.

③ An index is not a fund

Funds aim to follow an index, but because of fees (management fees) and other costs, they never match it exactly.
